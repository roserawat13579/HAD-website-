Who we are

Humla Association of the Deaf (HAD) is a district-level, non-profit, public welfare organisation established in 2074 B.S. (2017 A.D.) in Humla — one of the most geographically isolated districts in Nepal. HAD is registered with the District Administration Office and has been affiliated with the Nepal Social Welfare Council since February 28, 2018.

HAD works across all seven rural municipalities of Humla District on sign-language training, capacity building, livelihood support, quality education for deaf children, and advocacy for their basic rights — with the goal of helping children who have been neglected or abandoned by family and society become self-reliant, independent citizens.

"Children with hearing disabilities shall have the right to receive education through sign language." — Constitution of Nepal, Article 31(4)

The reality in Humla

A district-wide survey by HAD identified roughly 100 people with intellectual disabilities and 50 who are deaf or hard of hearing across Humla. Twenty of the district's deaf and hard-of-hearing children — neglected or abandoned by family and community — are now under HAD's care, taught in a single special resource class attached to Shree Dulli Primary School, Sarkegad-4.

The children currently live in a single rented room shared with general school students, without enough beds, blankets, safe drinking water, toilets, or basic hygiene items — and HAD has struggled to cover even the NPR 10,000 monthly rent. Given Humla's terrain, a residential school is the only realistic way to reach these children at all.

— Dharmadatta Phadera, President, Humla Association of the Deaf

What HAD provides — and wants to expand

With adequate support, HAD's residential school model covers the full range of a child's needs, not just a classroom seat.

01

Inclusive education for deaf and disabled children

Free schooling, sign-language instruction, and learning materials for children currently shut out of mainstream classrooms.

02

Residential hostel and safe housing

A dedicated hostel building so children no longer share a single rented room with no privacy or adequate facilities.

03

Nepali Sign Language (NSL) training

Training for teachers, families, and the children themselves, alongside NSL-based learning materials.

04

Digital accessibility and assistive technology

Tools and technology that widen access to information and communication for deaf and hard-of-hearing students.

05

Livelihood and self-employment training

Vocational and practical skills training so young people can become self-reliant as adults.

06

Leadership development for deaf women and youth

Programs building confidence and leadership among deaf women and young people in the district.

07

Health, hygiene, and psychosocial support

Coordination with local health centres for regular check-ups, first aid, and wellbeing support for the children in HAD's care.

What stands in the way

HAD has applied repeatedly to government and non-government bodies; most requests have gone unanswered. These are the gaps that remain.

Housing

20 children live in one rented room at NPR 10,000/month, with more children needing shelter turned away for lack of space.

Basic facilities

Beds, blankets, mattresses, safe drinking water, toilets, and hygiene items are all in short supply.

Classroom space

Teaching happens in one room shared with general students, which limits both groups' learning environment.

Trained teachers

A single teacher instructs every age group together, without enough NSL-based teaching materials.

Staffing

Not enough hands to cook, clean, and care for children living on-site full time.

An office

No office space or equipment exists to manage records, health data, or admissions for the district's deaf community.

Reaching families

Humla's terrain makes data collection and outreach to remote villages slow and costly.
